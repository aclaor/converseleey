The one move that carries a conversation

6 min read·Cites peer-reviewed research·No signup

Most advice about being good at conversation is about being interesting. The research points somewhere much narrower and much easier to practise: what you do with the sentence the other person just finished.

People who describe themselves as bad at conversation can usually open one. What they can't do is keep it alive past the third exchange. The opener works, the answer comes back, and then there's a small void where the next thing was supposed to be.

The standard advice is to be more interesting — have better stories, know more things, develop a personality worth talking to. That advice is unactionable tonight, and there's evidence it's aimed at the wrong target entirely.

The question-asking studies

In 2017, Karen Huang, Michael Yeomans, Alison Wood Brooks, Julia Minson and Francesca Gino published research in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology looking at what predicts whether someone likes you after a conversation. They analysed real conversations — both with human coders and with machine learning over the transcripts.

The finding: people who ask more questions are better liked. And one type of question did most of the work.

Not questions in general. Follow-up questions — ones that ask the other person to say more about the thing they just said.

A follow-up question is defined narrowly here: a question that encourages your partner to elaborate on the content of their immediately preceding turn. Not your next prepared question. Not a related question on the same topic. A question about the specific thing they just told you.

Then the researchers took it somewhere with real stakes.

Huang et al., 2017 — the speed-dating study
110participants across a total of 1,961 four-minute speed dates
4minutes per date — barely enough time to ask anything at all
+8%a difference of this much in follow-up question rate predicted roughly one additional second-date request across a 20-date evening

An eight percent shift in one specific behaviour, measured over four-minute conversations, changed how many people wanted to see you again. Not charisma. Not what you did for a living. Whether you asked about the thing they'd just said.

Why it works

The researchers tested the mechanism, and it wasn't that question-askers seemed cleverer or more curious in the abstract. Follow-up questions made people seem responsive — understanding, validating, and caring about the other person — and that perception is what drove the liking.

Which makes sense once you see what a follow-up question proves. Any question can be prepared in advance. A follow-up question is unforgeable evidence that you were listening to the last thirty seconds, because it could not have been written before they spoke.

That's the whole trick. You're not demonstrating that you're interesting. You're demonstrating that you were present, and you're doing it in a way that can't be faked.

How to actually do it

The mechanical version, which works even when your mind is blank:

Take the most specific noun

Find the most concrete thing in what they just said and ask about that.

  • They say: "We drove back from Penang last week."
    Weak: "Do you travel a lot?" — a topic question, could have been asked before they spoke.
    Strong: "You drove? How long is that?" — about the specific thing they said.
  • They say: "Work's been mad since the reorg."
    Weak: "What do you do?"
    Strong: "What changed in the reorg?"
  • They say: "I've been trying to get back into running."
    Weak: "Do you do any other sports?"
    Strong: "Back into — what stopped you?"

In every pair, the weak version moves to a new topic and the strong version goes one layer deeper into theirs. The weak version makes them start again. The strong version lets them continue.

Answer in two sentences, not one

The other half of this. When you're the one answering, give the answer plus one concrete detail they could grab onto — then stop. One-word answers force the other person to do all the work of finding the next question. Five sentences leave them nothing to ask about because you've closed every loop yourself.

Two sentences hands them a hook.

Let the silence sit for one beat

Follow-up questions need a moment to form, because by definition you couldn't have prepared them. If you fill every gap instantly, you never give yourself the second it takes to notice what was interesting in what they said.

What this doesn't mean

It doesn't mean interrogating people. The speed-dating result came from a modest increase in follow-up questions inside normal conversation, not from turning a date into a deposition. A conversation that's all questions in one direction stops being a conversation, and the other person will feel it.

Roughly: one follow-up per topic, then offer something of your own, then let them ask you something. The point isn't volume. It's that when you do ask, you ask about their last sentence rather than reaching for your next prepared one.

The reason this is worth practising deliberately is that it's the single most transferable habit in conversation. It works at a party, in an interview, on a date, and in a meeting where you need someone to feel heard before they'll move. And unlike being interesting, you can start doing it in the next conversation you have.
